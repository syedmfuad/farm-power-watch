Farm Power Watch

Do agricultural electricity prices rise faster where more data-center capacity is operating? This tool reports the association found in a study of realized agricultural prices (33 providers, 2012–2025) and illustrates it by utility and county. Methods & caveats

!
Read this first. The relationship below is an association, not an established causal effect, and its 95% confidence interval includes zero. The study (v18) finds the agricultural price rose faster after 2022 where more data-center capacity operates, but the magnitude is imprecise and unstable across comparisons, and agriculture does not respond more than commercial customers. Dollar figures show what the association would imply if it were causal — they are not established costs.

Methods & caveats

association figures come from the study's preferred controlled specification: how the growth in the realized agricultural electricity price (revenue ÷ sales on agricultural rate schedules in FERC Form 1, through 2025) relates to operating data-center capacity, measured as the coefficient on the log of one plus operating megawatts. The panel is 33 providers in 22 states (30 investor-owned utilities, 3 cooperatives). The controlled 2022–2025 coefficient is 0.0200, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.0025 to +0.0424. At the study's reference capacity of 364 MW this implies +12.5% greater price growth than at zero capacity (interval −1.4% to +28.4%).

Why "not established." The interval includes zero; the magnitude is unstable across credible comparisons (0.0233 on schedule families present at both endpoints, but only ~0.008 when the sample is restricted to providers with unchanged schedules or to near-miss comparison utilities); part of the measured rise reflects lower electricity use spreading fixed and demand charges over fewer kWh; and agriculture does not show a clearly larger response than commercial customers of the same utilities. The study identifies an agricultural input-price concern but does not establish a cross-subsidy from farms to data centers. An earlier version of this tool used posted tariffs (NREL USURDB); the study shows that apparent effect is largely an artifact of how often the rate database re-observes different utilities, so it has been replaced with the realized-price result.

scenario figures apply the same association to a larger operating capacity: either this utility's planned capacity (Cleanview; 25% realized by 2028, 50% by 2030), its state's planned-capacity trajectory, or LBNL/DOE national load-growth scenarios (176 TWh in 2023 → 325/450/580 TWh in 2028). They are illustrative, not forecasts.
